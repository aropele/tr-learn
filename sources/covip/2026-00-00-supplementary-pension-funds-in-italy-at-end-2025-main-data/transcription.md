## Overview (pp. 1–3)

In 2025, the Italian supplementary pension system proved resilient despite persistent geopolitical tensions and continued uncertainty in the global economic environment. Membership and contributions continued to grow, while assets increased driven by the positive performance of financial markets.

By the end of 2025, more than 10 million members had joined Italian pension funds, representing a 4 percent increase compared with the previous year. Total net assets under management reached 262 billion euro (7.7 percent higher than 2024), representing 11.6 percent of GDP and 4 percent of household financial assets.

*Note (transcriber): the "4 percent" membership increase here differs from the 4.8 percent stated below and in Tables 1 and 4; kept as printed.*

\*   \*   \*

### Number of pension funds

As of the end of 2025, there were 273 pension funds in total: 33 contractual pension funds, 38 open pension funds, 71 'new' PIPs (see Glossary) and 131 pre-existing pension funds (of which 96 were autonomous pension funds).

The number of pension funds has been steadily declining in recent years, with a decrease of 18 funds in 2025 alone and 446 since 2000, reflecting an ongoing consolidation process that has primarily affected pre-existing pension funds. As a result, the average size of pension funds has increased, triggering economies of scale that can translate into lower costs for members.

### Membership

The more than 10 million members of Italian pension funds were distributed as follows: 4.4 million in contractual pension funds, 2.2 million in open pension funds, 3.8 million in 'new' PIPs, approximately 666,000 in pre-existing pension funds and 272,000 in 'old' PIPs (see Glossary).

In 2025, the total number of members increased by 4.8 percent compared with the previous year. Growth rates varied across pension fund types: 6.1 percent for contractual pension funds, 8.7 percent for open pension funds, 2.9 percent for 'new' PIPs and 0.9 percent for pre-existing pension funds.

The participation rate in the supplementary pension system stood at 39.9 percent of the labour force, falling to 29.0 percent when excluding members who made no contributions to their individual accounts during the year.

Among all members, 7.7 million were employees, 1.2 million were self-employed workers and 1.5 million fell into the 'other' category (see Glossary).

In terms of gender, men accounted for 61.2 percent of total members. Gender gaps were particularly pronounced in contractual pension funds, where women represented only 28.3 percent of participants. By contrast, the gender distribution was more balanced in open pension funds and PIPs.

As regards age, 45.5 percent of members were between 35 and 54 years old, while 33.7 percent were aged 55 or over. The average age of members was 46.9 years, with no significant gender differences.

With regard to the geographical area of residence, most members lived in Northern Italy (57.3 percent).

### Contributions, benefits and asset accumulation

In 2025, contributions collected by Italian pension funds amounted to 22.4 billion euro (increasing by 8.6 percent compared with 2024). Of this amount, 18.7 billion were allocated into employees' accounts, including: 5.7 billion from employee contributions, 3.4 from employer contributions and 9.6 from the TFR flows (see Glossary) into pension funds. Contributions from self-employed workers were 1.9 billion euro; while the remaining 1.6 billion euro came from other members (see Glossary).

*Note (transcriber): Table 1 prints the change in total contributions as 8.7 percent, not 8.6; kept as printed.*

Total benefits reached 14 billion euro. Lump-sum pension benefits amounted to 5.5 billion euro, while 810 million euro were in the form of annuities. Early payments, called RITA (see Glossary), totalled 2.8 billion euro. Redemptions and early withdrawals amounted to approximately 2.1 billion and 2.8 billion euro, respectively.

Total net assets available for retirement provision (including those held at insurance undertakings) amounted to 262 billion euro, an increase of 7.7 percent compared with 2024. Net assets rose by 9.3 percent (to 81.5 billion euro) for contractual pension funds, 14 percent (to 42.5 billion) for open pension funds, 8 percent (to 59.1 billion) for 'new' PIPs and 3.2 percent (to 71.9 billion) for pre-existing pension funds. Net assets held by 'old' PIPs amounted to 7 billion euro.

### Asset allocation

At the end of 2025, pension fund assets were invested primarily in debt securities (55.8 percent), mostly government bonds. Additionally, 24 percent were invested in equities and 15.3 percent in mutual funds.

Domestic investments accounted for 19.3 percent of total assets (44 billion euro), the majority of which consisted of government bonds. Investments in securities issued by Italian companies remained limited: 5.8 billion euro (2.6 percent of total assets), of which 3.4 billion were in corporate bonds and the remainder in equities.

### Returns and costs

In 2025, returns on pension funds, net of costs and taxes, were, on average, 4.8 percent for contractual pension funds and 5.7 percent for open pension funds. Among 'new' PIPs, the returns of unit-linked products were 5.1 percent, while those of with-profit products (valued at historical cost rather than market value) were 1.5 percent. The TFR revaluation, net of taxes, was 1.9 percent.

Equity-oriented investment options outperformed other investment options, delivering returns of 7.7 percent for contractual pension funds, 9.6 percent for open pension funds and 7.8 percent for unit-linked 'new' PIPs.

Over a 10-year investment horizon (2015-2025), the average compounded annual return, net of costs and taxes, was 2.4 percent for contractual pension funds and 2.7 percent for open pension funds. Among 'new' PIPs, unit-linked products delivered a return of 3.1 percent, while with-profit products achieved a return of 1.5 percent. The average annual TFR revaluation, net of taxes, was 2.5 percent.

Over the same horizon, equity-oriented investment options delivered 10-year returns ranging from 4.8 to 5.1 percent across all types of pension funds (outperforming both bond-oriented investment options and TFR over the same period). Balanced investment options yielded average returns ranging from 1.9 percent for unit-linked PIPs to 2.7 percent in contractual pension funds and 2.9 percent in open pension funds.

Costs vary significantly across different types of pension funds and are particularly dispersed among open pension funds, and especially within PIPs. For a representative member with a 10-year holding period, the average Synthetic Cost Indicator (SCI – calculated according to the common methodology set by COVIP – see Glossary) is 0.47 percent for contractual pension funds, 1.36 percent for open pension funds and 2.17 percent for 'new' PIPs.

## Glossary (pp. 28–29)

**Contractual Pension Funds (CPFs):** pension funds established through collective bargaining agreements between employer associations and trade unions, generally negotiated at industry level or, in some cases, with reference to specific geographical areas. CPFs only support occupational pension plans and have legal personality (i.e., the pension fund is legally separated from the sponsoring entities).

**Open Pension Funds (OPFs):** pension funds promoted by banks, insurance companies, asset management companies and investment companies. They support both occupational and personal pension plans. They do not have legal personality; nonetheless, their assets must be kept separate from those of the financial company managing them.

**'New' PIPs:** individual pension plans implemented through life insurance contracts and established after Legislative Decree No. 252 of 2005 entered into force. Offered by insurance companies, they only support personal pension plans. They do not have legal personality; nonetheless, their assets must be kept separate from those of the sponsoring insurance company. They may be either with-profit products (traditional policies) or unit-linked policies.

**'Old' PIPs:** individual pension plans implemented through life insurance contracts and established before to Legislative Decree No. 252 of 2005 entered into force. They are closed to new adhesions.

**Pre-existing Pension Funds (Old Pension Funds):** pension funds that were already operating before Legislative Decree No. 124 of 1993 entered into force. They include both autonomous and non-autonomous funds (book reserves). Pre-existing pension funds are exempt from certain rules applicable to pension funds established after 1993; in particular, they may directly manage their assets and invest in real estate. They may also continue to operate as defined benefit schemes; however, in this case they are closed to new adhesions.

**RITA (temporary early supplementary income):** RITA is an option available to members in disadvantageous conditions (e.g., termination of employment, unemployment for more than 24 months) who are close to reaching retirement age. It allows members to access the capital accumulated in their individual pension account and receive a temporary income stream until they become eligible for old-age pension payments. Members may choose the amount of accumulated capital to be used for RITA.

**Synthetic Cost Indicator (SCI):** an indicator aimed at easily communicating all the costs charged to a member (during the accumulation phase), expressed as a percentage of the assets held in their individual account. The SCI must be calculated according to the methodology established by COVIP. The SCI for each investment option must be disclosed in the information document to be provided to members (Le informazioni chiave per l'aderente) and on the COVIP website – where an interactive tool (https://www.covip.it/isc_dinamico/) makes it easier for users to compare the SCI of different pension schemes.

**TFR (Trattamento di fine rapporto):** the TFR is the severance payment that an employer must pay to an employee upon termination of employment or retirement. To this end, the employer sets aside each month 6.91 percent of the employee's gross salary. The accumulated TFR, which is recorded as a book reserve on the employer's balance sheet, is revalued annually according to a CPI-linked formula.

**Individual accounts:** the number of accounts held with pension funds. This figure may differ from the number of members, as a single member may hold more than one individual account.

**Other members:** individuals who either no longer meet the eligibility requirements for participating in their pension fund or who have reached the legal retirement requirements under the compulsory social security system; individuals who are fiscally dependent on others; and all other individuals not belonging to any of the categories listed above.

**Legislative Decree No. 147 of 2018:** implementation of Directive (EU) 2016/2341 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 14 December 2016 on the activities and supervision of institutions for occupational retirement provision (IORPs), commonly referred to as the 'IORP II Directive'.

**Legislative Decree No. 124 of 1993:** the first piece of legislation regulating the establishment and operation of new pension funds (contractual pension funds and open pension funds – see above) and defining the tasks and powers of the Pension Funds Supervisory Authority (Commissione di Vigilanza sui Fondi Pensione – COVIP). It was fully replaced by Legislative Decree No. 252 of 2005.

**Legislative Decree No. 252 of 2005:** the main legislative act for the pension fund sector, currently in place.
