## A Tri-Polar World

*Bridgewater Daily Observations, December 20, 2019. Karen Karniol-Tambour, Melissa Saphier, Ascanio Guarini.*

As the old saying goes, "When the US sneezes, the whole world catches a cold." That's gradually changing. Economic might has become more diffuse across the globe. Today, the US, Europe, and China each contribute similar amounts of global output and demand, together accounting for half of the global total. All three act as independent economic engines, powered by monetary and fiscal policy that is set based on their own needs. When growth in one of these economies picks up or stalls, there is a ripple around the world via their purchases from abroad. Each of these economies is most interconnected with its regional neighbors, but they are also interdependent, with sizable economic relationships outside their regions. Additionally, each economy has its own particular way that it exerts a major influence on outcomes beyond its own borders. The dollar is the world's primary reserve currency, so shifts in the Fed's monetary policy have disproportionate impacts on borrowing rates around the world. Europe is the largest direct exporter of capital, and China is the largest contributor to global growth. This tri-polar world provides investors with a tremendous opportunity for deeper diversification by spreading exposure across the engines of global economic activity.

One way of examining global demand is by looking at where global businesses sell their goods around the world. Twenty years ago, sales to China were small and mostly came from domestic companies. Since then, China's purchases of global companies' goods and services have overtaken those to Europe. Today, the US, Europe, and China together account for half of global listed company sales. Foreign companies account for nearly 40% of sales to each of these economies, reflecting the interconnected nature of the global economy. In the chart below, the height of each column shows how much of total global listed company sales are purchased by each economy, and the color coding shows where these companies are listed.

> **Figure 1** (p. 3). USA/EUR/CHN Share of Global Listed Company Sales, by Origin.
> Stacked columns for USA, EUR and CHN in two panels, "20 Yrs Ago: Two main economic engines" and "Today: A tri-polar world", colored by where the selling companies are listed (Americas, Europe, Asia, Other; segments labeled e.g. USA, CAN, EUR, GBR, JPN, CHN, Other Asia). Twenty years ago: USA about 25% (mostly US-listed), EUR about 19% (mostly European), CHN about 4%. Today: USA about 21%, EUR about 14%, CHN about 17% (mostly Chinese-listed, with other Asian and some European companies).

The US, Europe, and China combined also account for about half of global imports, with each being a similar sized engine of demand for other countries around the world. The US and Europe import significant amounts from their regional neighbors, but they are also highly integrated globally, with significant imports from each other's regions as well as Asia. China, usually thought of as an export powerhouse, is importing about as much as Europe—although it buys largely from other Asian economies. (Note that we do not include exports from one European country to another, in the same way that we don't show trade between US states or Chinese provinces.)

**Each economy has additional channels through which it exerts significant influence on outcomes beyond its own borders.** As shown below, China is the largest contributor to global growth, so global businesses perceive it as a unique source of growth for their businesses. Europe is the largest direct exporter of capital, and the dollar is the world's primary reserve currency such that the Fed's monetary policy has disproportionate impacts on borrowing rates around the world.

**Zooming in on capital flows,** while the US and Europe still dominate portfolio outflows, China is quickly catching up in its impact on other countries through FDI and banking flows. Portfolio outflows, or purchases of financial assets like stocks and bonds, are still limited given that China remains in the process of gradually liberalizing its capital account and is no longer building up large reserve positions as it was from the mid-2000s until about 2015. But China is increasingly impactful through other outflows, such as its strategic outward investment program the Belt and Road Initiative.

**The dollar remains the dominant global currency.** Most cross-border debt is dollar debt, so when the Fed eases borrowers around the world feel the impact of that easing, and vice versa when the Fed tightens. The only other currency where there is meaningful cross-border borrowing is the euro, which accounts for just 20% of total cross-border liabilities, concentrated in the Eurozone's neighboring countries. The majority of global trade is also invoiced in dollars, another reflection of its dominant impact on global cross-border transactions.

**China is the largest contributor to global growth.** Although its share of global demand (a sum of consumption and investment) is still lower than the US and roughly on par with Europe, it is growing much faster. That means that it will become a larger share of the global economy through time, and it provides a faster-growing market compared to the slow growth in the developed world.

**This tri-polar world presents a valuable opportunity for global diversification,** but so far investors' geographic allocations have lagged these shifts. As shown below, across economies institutional portfolios have significant home country bias, with their home country's assets comprising the majority of the risk in most cases. The foreign allocation tends to be dominated by the US, and there is little diversification beyond just two countries.

Global portfolio exposure to China in particular is tiny, though it is growing as Chinese markets gradually open up, making significant geographic diversification easier for investors to achieve.

## Grappling with the New Reality of Zero Bond Yields Virtually Everywhere — Part 1: The Facts and the Implications

*Bridgewater Daily Observations, July 13, 2020. Bob Prince, Greg Jensen, Melissa Saphier.*

It is now a reality that long-term bond yields are at or near zero in the US and virtually everywhere. There are so many implications of this that it takes some time to recognize and absorb them all and then more time to work through what to do about it, which is what we've been doing and what we see the biggest and most sophisticated institutional investors doing as well. Given the status of the US dollar as the primary reserve currency and US bonds as the "risk-free asset," having the US bond yield at or near zero goes beyond the implications for bonds the asset because the interest rate is the price of credit and is the discount rate on all other cash flows. A zero interest rate effectively means that there is no interest rate, and if it stays at zero, it means no change in the interest rate. Thus, any asset or any form of credit that is impacted by the level or the change in the interest rate is impacted, which extends to all economies and markets and the policies that drive them.

For us to say that we are here is something, since, as you know, we've explained for a decade or more why *low* bond yields are not really a problem, and during this time, we've put low bond yields to good use in beta and in alpha. But we've now crossed a line, and we all have to deal with this new reality.

Among the questions that this raises are the following:

- How much room is left for bonds to rally?
- What is the potential to earn a risk premium by holding leveraged bonds funded by cash?
- What are the asymmetries pertaining to the range of potential bond returns?
- For portfolios that include equities and bonds, how much support is lost from bonds no longer being able to cushion a decline in equities?
- How does the downside of equities and other assets change if the discount rate on cash flows can't fall?
- For the economy and earnings, how does this impact the ability to cushion or pull out of a downturn?
- How does this impact the operation of monetary and fiscal policy?
- What are the portfolio implications, and what can be done to make portfolios more resilient?

Given such questions, in one way or another, the zero bond yield has been the gravitational center of our research in recent months, and we've both made adjustments and developed new insights related to it. The purpose of this *Observations* and the next two is to walk through some of this. The topics are obviously deep and complex. Today, we will just touch on these questions and then in the next couple of days begin to get into the what-do-you-do-about-it. Before we go to the specifics, the following chart showing US bond yields since 1800 starts to convey the uniqueness of the current circumstances.

> **Figure 11** (p. 8). USA Nominal 10yr Bond Yield.
> Line from 1800 to 2020. Yields mostly range between about 2% and 7% through the 19th and early 20th centuries, rise to a peak of about 16% around 1981, then fall to below 1% in 2020, the lowest in the series; a horizontal arrow at the bottom runs from today back to 1800 to show the current level has no precedent.

**The Uniqueness of a Zero Bond Yield and No Risk Premium/Yield Curve Slope versus Cash**

Not only is the bond yield lower than ever, this is the first time it's been low with a flat yield curve. For example, in 2009 when the short-term interest rate was zero, the yield curve slope was about 3%, and in 1933 when short rates were zero and the Fed started printing, the bond yield was 3.5%. So in those cases, even though yields were low and the long-term expected return of holding bonds was similarly low, the potential for excess returns in leveraged bonds was very high due to the implied rise in bond yields as reflected in those steep yield curves and the long duration of the bonds. Today, we have long durations but little or no rise priced in, little or no risk premium versus cash, and obviously a low expected total return of holding those bonds.

> **Figure 12** (p. 8). Long Rate and Short Rate (US), 1900–2020 (untitled chart).
> Long rate (blue) and short rate (red). Short rates were near zero in the 1930s–40s and after 2008 while long rates stayed well above them; both peak around 1980–81 (short rate about 17%). A circle marks 2020, where both lines have converged near 0%, i.e. no curve slope.

Today, we have a lower starting point and less opportunity for yields to decline relative to what is discounted to further boost returns. While one can't say for sure how low yields could go, the obvious limitation is that at a certain level, cash hoarding becomes a more attractive alternative. Given the frictions between the central bank policy rate and the rates facing other borrowers and lenders (we would guess around -1%), policy rates would be unlikely to trigger a move to cash in most countries. Below that point, it becomes less clear. And at least for now, central bankers across the world have expressed growing hesitancy about further use of negative rates as a policy tool, in particular focusing on the potential adverse effects for the banking system, which could weaken the efficacy of such policies.

**What are the asymmetries in the range of potential bond returns in this environment?**

With limited room for yields to fall and no limit on how much they can rise, the distribution of potential returns for bonds and rates is adversely skewed. Of course, looking back there are underlying secular forces that have pulled yields down, and in practice, how yields evolve from here will depend on how economic conditions unfold, how policy makers respond, and how that impacts investor preferences. Considering the range of outcomes looking out over the next three years, a "best case" bond rally to -1% would bring bond returns to a cumulative 17%. Whereas if we were to see real yields return to their long-term average (a little over 2%) and a moderate rise in inflation to 4%, that would produce about -30% returns over the three years.

This year, we got a glimpse of what it looks like when bond yields are already floored when a downturn arrives. While US bonds had room to fall and produce strong returns, there was much less support in Europe and none in Japan.

Normally when economic conditions are deteriorating and equities are falling, a bottom is formed when the central bank steps in and provides enough easing to offset these negative pressures. This supports equities in two ways: the support to the economy helps stabilize earnings prospects, and the declining discount rate pushes up the present value of future earnings. Zero nominal yields also create a unique linkage between real yields and inflation. Because there is an arbitrage between the breakeven inflation rate and actual inflation, a deflationary downturn that pushes breakeven inflation down is extra risky because the combination pushes real yields up as the economy contracts (because the real yield plus breakeven inflation must equal the nominal yield, and the nominal yield is relatively stable), i.e., you have a higher discount rate on cash flows as cash flows fall. On the other hand, if reflation is successful, central banks will likely delay the rise in nominal yields relative to inflation, forcing real yields to fall. And there is no lower limit to either real yields or breakeven inflation. As a result, a successful reflation can drive real yields much lower even if they start at low levels, and policy failure (i.e., deflation) will drive them higher.

As shown below, Japan has experienced these dynamics since the '90s. Before rates reached the zero lower bound (marked below with a gray vertical line), inflation and short rates fell and rose together, reflecting the central bank's responsiveness to conditions. But after rates reached zero, the relationship inverted. Falling inflation, when rates are already at zero, forces real yields higher, producing a tightening as conditions are deteriorating.

> **Figure 17** (p. 13). Japan: Core Inflation (Y/Y) and Real Short Rate.
> Lines 1985–2020. ## Engineering Equity Return Streams: Top-Down, Bottom-Up

*Bridgewater Daily Observations, November 30, 2020. Bob Prince.*

The world of zero interest rates presents a number of investment challenges and in our view calls for new ways of thinking in order to create new betas, new alphas, and new ways of constructing portfolios. Although there are no new asset classes, there is the potential to create new beta and alpha return streams from what exists. This is possible because macro conditions are manifest in the spending and incomes of businesses, creating the potential to engineer new cash flow streams from collections of companies whose cash flows and pricing are fundamentally driven by specified macro conditions. The returns of these engineered equity portfolios can be further refined by combining them with hedges or diversifying exposures in other liquid markets, and this can be done either passively or actively. The net result can be newly engineered return streams to create portfolios which have less risk at the same or higher returns as traditional equity or multi-asset portfolios, without holding nominal bonds.

One way that we have applied this way of thinking is through what we refer to as stable cash flow equities. The goal is to engineer a stable cash flow stream from equities in order to replicate some of the properties of a bond. The process starts with identifying stable and reliable types of spending in the economy, connecting that spending to the revenues received by the companies that offer those goods and services, and then screening for a relatively unobstructed passage of that revenue to earnings. The selected companies are effectively used as pass-through vehicles to earn a sliver of the cash flow stream generated by stable forms of spending in the economy. The constituents of the portfolio rotate over time to continuously source their income stream from the associated types of spending, creating a new cash flow stream that you can hold passively or manage tactically. Below is what that cash flow stream looks like compared to the cash flow stream of the overall equity market in the US since 1963 and in other developed countries since 1990.

> **Figure 18** (p. 14). Cumulative Op Inc per Share (ln, Simulated). Footnote 1.

Dev Wld ex-USA, 1990–2020: the stable series rises fairly steadily from about 0.1 to about 0.8, while the total market swings widely, from about -0.7 in the early 1990s to about 0.8 before 2008, collapsing around 2009 and again in 2020.

Given the stability of this cash flow stream, the returns of the portfolio take on a more bond-like character, which we estimate to be roughly 70% stocks and 30% bonds. The returns are less growth-sensitive, more inflation-sensitive, and less sensitive to risk premiums and tight money than the overall equity market. Because the cash flows are stable and reasonably predictable, price changes mostly reflect changes in market discount rates and imply a change in expected yield rather than a change in future earnings. This makes price changes substantially hedgeable through liquid market proxies that reflect these discount rates (e.g., short bonds, long breakeven inflation, etc.) of the portfolio. The net effect of stabilizing the cash flows and hedging changes in the discount rates reduces the volatility of the portfolio and reduces the impact of changes in the macro environment on returns. Another example of this engineering process pertains to stable balance sheets. Whereas stable cash flow equities are designed to have a stable income stream, resilient companies have balance sheets and operating characteristics which enable them to emerge intact after periods of severe economic stress, even if they experience substantial declines in sales and earnings. At the opposite end of this spectrum are susceptible companies that have vulnerable balance sheets and operating characteristics, leading to permanent impairment from economic stress.

Companies are continuously assessed for their balance sheet resilience by running pro forma cash flow projections under stress conditions and sorting them according to how well their liquidity conditions hold up. From a portfolio standpoint, spreading one against the other isolates extreme differences in financial durability whose impact scales as a function of the depth and duration of the stress environment. This creates a recession hedge cash flow stream that is negatively correlated to changes in risk premiums without paying the risk premium, making it quite valuable from a portfolio-construction standpoint.

For susceptible companies, stress environments often lead to a big shift in the proportionate claim of debt holders relative to equity holders as debts rise relative to market cap. For resilient companies, the relative claims remain stable.

In both of these applications, the cash flows associated with macroeconomic spending conditions are pushed through companies' income statements and balance sheets in order to engineer a new set of cash flow and financial characteristics that meets specific parameters with respect to the macro environment. This creates new betas which can then be held or actively managed to create new alphas. These betas can be combined and held passively, producing reliable forms of diversification rooted in the nature of the underlying cash flows. Or they can be actively managed based on views of macro conditions and how this will impact one in relation to another. And this can be done without holding or trading nominal bonds.

This top-down, bottom-up engineering process can be used to produce other betas according to other specifications. The starting point is an understanding of how economic conditions impact spending and how that spending impacts the cash flows and balance sheets of companies, making the approach equally applicable to private and public assets.

## MP3 Will Likely Be Pushed Until It Produces Excesses

*Bridgewater Daily Observations, March 18, 2021. Greg Jensen.*

With a strong US recovery in sight as the pandemic fades and a massive second round of fiscal stimulus underway, the Fed is nearing the point where it will face a difficult trade-off between growth/asset prices and inflation. We believe a mark of the new paradigm we are in is that the Fed (and policy makers elsewhere) will likely lag the economy much more than it has in decades, and it will continue lagging until that path encounters a limit. This is because pushing these policies is in some sense necessary, given the secular and societal challenges of too many promises and too much wealth relative to incomes and too much wealth concentrated in the hands of too few. The Fed decision on Wednesday and Powell's dovish comments were consistent with intentionally staying behind the curve. Huge QE remains in place despite the Fed forecast of a 4.5% unemployment by year-end and pockets of equity market froth. And most FOMC members are not signalizing a desire or expectation to raise rates before 2024.

While being necessary, these policies are not without risk. In fact, we are confident that these policies will result in growing excesses of some kind—whether it's inflation, currency weakness, and/or asset bubbles. Inflation is likely an important part of the most likely endgame. And while we could be wrong about that being the excess that materializes, the risk of higher inflation is high enough that it has to be considered and planned for in strategic portfolios, which by and large remain positioned for a continuation of low and stable inflation.

Below, we bring together our thoughts on how we see MP3 policies playing out over time and the excesses we think they will eventually produce:

1. We are in a new policy paradigm, with the end of preemptive inflation fighting and the shift to Monetary Policy 3/MP3 (the fusion of monetary and fiscal policy) as the primary policy tool.
2. MP3 is inherently more inflationary than past policies because it injects so much demand into the real economy without corresponding supply, the response has been rapid and massive, and more is on the way in the US.
3. With the likely surge in growth as the virus fades and economies reopen, the Fed is approaching the point where it may face difficult trade-offs, but the incentives are skewed toward lagging the economy.
4. Longer term, higher inflation (probably via wages) is <u>desirable</u> from a policy perspective, as it is the least painful way to resolve the basic challenge of too little income relative to claims on income in the form of a) future IOUs and b) current wealth.
5. De facto debt monetization eventually leading to inflation is the classic playbook and is already underway: this is the modern-day jubilee.
6. Most of the major secular forces that have supported a secular downwave in inflation since 1980 are at their limits or turning. The deflationary impact of technology/automation alone (which is arguably slowing) would have to outweigh the fading of these other forces to support continued low inflation.

With respect to inflation risk, **the policies that are now in place and are being conducted on a huge scale will produce inflation if pushed far enough**. In the extreme, imagine if everyone stopped working and producing goods and services and the government replaced all of that lost income with printed money—there's no question that would be massively inflationary. The critical question is how far the policies will be pushed. As we'll explain, there's a need to push them pretty far—and a real opportunity to address societal challenges in the process. And the policies feel great in the moment and are enacted (or not) by political bodies that have to face regular elections. We would bet that the limits of these policies will be tested.

**We Are in a New Policy Paradigm: The End of Preemptive Inflation Fighting and the Shift to MP3**

We have previously described the secular debt dynamics that have produced falling interest rates since the early 1980s, with every peak in interest rates lower than the prior peak and every trough lower than the prior trough. Due to rising debt levels, it took more and more easing with each cyclical downturn to achieve the same reduction in debt burdens and the same amount of stimulation, and it took tightening to lower and lower levels to achieve the same amount of slowdown. What is remarkable is that **since 2000, government spending in the US has started to take on a similar profile**, with the deficit surging to greater and greater levels with each downturn and receding to generally higher levels even as the economy recovered. The appetite for deficit spending has been increasing for some time.

**MP3 Is Inherently More Inflationary Than Past Policies, the Response Has Been Rapid and Massive, and More Is on the Way in the US**

When central banks shifted to QE after the 2008 crisis there were widespread fears that it would be inflationary, but we didn't think it would be. The reason it wasn't is that central banks were acting to offset a collapse in credit, which by itself was highly deflationary, and were simply replacing lost credit with printed money. **MP3 is inherently different and inherently more inflationary: governments are spending directly in the real economy without creating corresponding supply.** The Fed can act as a bank, by making up for lost credit; it cannot act as a restaurant, by making up for those lost goods and services. In the acute contraction following the pandemic shutdowns last year, the inflationary effects of MP3 were offset by the deflationary impact of that contraction. But as these policies remain even as economies are recovering, that balance begins to tip.

It's worth putting in perspective the speed of the policy response we have seen, as it could portend the speed of the real economy response as the virus fades and economies reopen. What we saw was reflation in warp speed. After stocks crashed in the Great Depression, it took three years and seven months for QE to come online; that took one year and six months in the global financial crisis and only one month in the COVID-19 shock. From the start of the crisis until stocks approached new highs took nearly seven-and-a-half years in the Depression, two-and-a-half years in the global financial crisis, and three-and-a-half months during COVID-19.

**Longer Term, Higher Inflation Is <u>Desirable</u> from a Policy Perspective, as It Is the Least Painful Way to Resolve the Basic Challenge of Too Little Income**

There will always be cyclical wiggles in policy (including the potential for policy failure), but longer term it's hard to see any plausible path out of the secular challenges in the developed world, and the US in particular, other than higher inflation. **The basic challenge is one of too little income**—too little income relative to claims on income in the form of a) future IOUs and b) current wealth. Future IOUs (e.g., debt, pension liabilities, Medicare, social security) are direct draws on income, but wealth is also a draw on someone else's future income: equity valuations rest on future company earnings; bonds are a claim on the debtor's income; etc. **There are too many total claims on income relative to likely future incomes, and something will have to give.**

Below, we show total IOUs in the US relative to national income (GDP); as shown, IOUs have surged to nearly 10x incomes, and even prior to COVID-19 were projected to reach roughly 11.5x incomes by 2030. Similarly, given the massive surge in asset prices (paper wealth), household wealth as a percentage of incomes is at nearly 6x.

So what are the potential ways that this massive imbalance between likely incomes and the claims on incomes can be resolved? We see three potential paths:

1. A productivity boom that raises future incomes;
2. Defaulting on the promises/allowing a massive correction in asset prices; and
3. Higher inflation that raises nominal incomes and future nominal cash flows, bringing them in line with the nominal claims on those incomes.

A productivity boom is of course possible, but it's unclear where it would come from. Given the fact that defaulting on the promises would hit the least-advantaged members of society the hardest and levels of social division and political conflict are already high, that path doesn't look politically feasible, to say the least (i.e., such moves can sow the seeds of revolutions). Similarly, a big correction in asset prices would be painful and politically challenging. History has repeatedly shown that **the least painful path out of this type of challenge is inflation** to gradually ease debt burdens and sustain decent nominal returns (though worse real returns), effecting a slow and silent redistribution from creditors to debtors. **MP3 is the tool to do this**, and in particular can be used in a much more targeted way than MP1 or MP2 to direct spending to the people and places in the economy that need it most to start to deal with these and other societal challenges.

As another perspective on the level of paper wealth, we can measure asset prices in terms of how many years of future earnings companies have already promised to equity and debt holders. Today, the level of outstanding claims would take close to 25 years for companies to pay back. This is a near-record claim on future incomes and illustrates how high asset prices are relative to incomes. And when we look at how past peaks were resolved, it was generally either through a fall in price (as in Great Depression or the bursting of the dot-com bubble) or a rise in inflation and poor nominal returns (as in the late '60s /early '70s).

**De Facto Debt Monetization Eventually Leading to Inflation Is the Classic Playbook and Is Already Underway: The Modern-Day Jubilee**

When we talk about the significance of the recent shift to MP3, it can obscure the fact that **we have been proceeding down a policy path to deal with unsustainable debt burdens for some time, a path whose endgame is typically higher inflation**, with MP3 just the latest tool to conduct that policy. Since the developed world reached its debt limits in the financial crisis, developed world policy has gradually created a very managed de facto extinguishment of debt through monetization—a modern-day jubilee—and that process was accelerated by the pandemic crisis.

The last jubilee occurred in the 1930s–'40s, so it's instructive to compare the arc of the jubilee then to where we are in the process today. Then, as now, the jubilee had two distinct phases—an initial printing and spending push in the early '30s that was then dwarfed by the giant stimulus of the war effort. The modern-day jubilee begins when private sector debt becomes insurmountable. Even pushing interest rates as low as they can go is not enough to compel a robust upswing in borrowing and investment to power the economy. So central banks transition from MP1 (interest rates) to MP2 (QE).

Today (1990–2021): rates fall to near zero after 2008 and again in 2020 while the monetary base rises from about 6% to about 22% by about 2015 and to about 25% in 2020. **Money printing finances either the direct purchase of private sector debts or spending that supports private sector incomes (i.e., MP3)** and facilitates a private sector deleveraging. **Either way, the result is the same: a transfer of private sector debts to the government balance sheet.**

**Most of the Major Secular Disinflationary Forces since 1980 Are Turning; Technology/Automation Alone Would Have to Support Continued Disinflation**

When we step back and take stock of the major forces that have produced a secular wave of disinflation since 1980, it looks to us like most are either at their limits or turning. Over the past 40 years, we first had Volcker's tight interest rate policy designed to break the back of inflation; now we have near-zero interest rates and governments printing money and spending it in the real economy (MP3). We had a paradigm of proactive tightening into tight inflation; now we have clear evidence of a shift away from preemptive tightening toward policies like average inflation targeting. We had a rapid increase in access to global markets and the development of global supply chains that drove down costs; we now have greater external conflict, risk of de-globalization, and a shift from efficient to resilient supply chains. We had an environment of policy favoring businesses over labor; we now have high levels of social division and internal conflict and growing populism that make such policies unsustainable. **The one secular disinflationary force that remains is technology and automation, which by itself would have to support lower inflation and offset the fading of these other forces.**

## What Markets Are Discounting

*Bridgewater Daily Observations, July 20, 2021. Greg Jensen, Jason Rotenberg, Kate Dunbar, Nikolai Doytchinov.*

*More a reflection of what policy makers deem necessary, and increasingly diverging from conditions.*

In today's *Observations*, we illustrate and describe what we see markets discounting. Before doing that, it is worth laying out how our thinking on market pricing has evolved over the last decade as economic policy has shifted. If you go back several decades, it was reasonable to think of asset prices as reflecting a largely private-sector-determined outlook for the value of the future cash flows that an asset would produce. So, interest rates roughly reflected anticipated growth and economic conditions, corporate spreads roughly reflected probabilities of default, and equity valuations roughly reflected expected growth rates and market interest rates. The MP3 policy world turns a lot of pricing on its head. It is probably more accurate to think of what level of interest rates will generate the economic outcomes that policy makers want than to think of rates as reflecting conditions in the economy. Credit spreads are more reflective of the liquidity and purchases of central banks determining the spreads that meet their policy goals than private-sector-formulated odds of default, although those things are related. Equities reflect market participants being driven out of interest-bearing products that are affected by the policies above. The big issue for all markets will be how long policy makers can sustain policies that are becoming increasingly divergent from the underlying economic conditions and incentives.

We will discuss in more detail our own market views and the areas where we disagree with what is priced in in future notes. Looking at conditions today and the pressures going forward, we see wider divergences between macro conditions and what is priced in than have existed for most of the last decade. In particular, the divergence between what is priced into US bonds and the strength of cyclical conditions, inflation and inflation pressures, and the looseness of fiscal policy is the widest since we started managing money in the 1970s.

Market pricing in aggregate is consistent with:

- **Continued near-zero rates indefinitely**, achieving only very modest growth and inflation outcomes.
- **A transitory inflation surge, with inflation rates reverting to, or falling below, central bank targets.**
- **This will be accomplished with low volatility.**
- **Continued US outperformance and EM underperformance.**
- **Low future returns, but a large spread between earnings yields and bonds.**
- **Pockets of excesses within the equity market not impacting the macro.**

When it comes to the recent bond market action, we believe we understand why the bond market is clearing at current prices and think that these prices are unlikely to be sustained. The drivers we see are:

1. **COVID fears driving concerns of renewed economic weakness, uncertainty, and an easier path for monetary policy.** We will hit our thoughts on COVID in an upcoming *Observations* and will adapt our views if these concerns begin to materialize in higher savings rates or new shutdowns. But, for now, we continue to see massive savings across the world that are more likely than not to be spent down.
2. **Market deference to the Fed's stated likely policy path.** As is always the case, the Fed will react to conditions even if its reaction function has changed. What matters most is how conditions play out.
3. **A lack of demand for credit from households and businesses that are flush with liquidity, and other flows dynamics**, which we think are likely to fade over time.

Before diving into the pricing, the charts below provide a quick snapshot of where we are and how that compares with the pricing. US levels of activity are back to pre-COVID trend, and inflation is running above. The discounting is for not much change in policy outside of unwinding QE. The rest of the developed world is a bit behind in the post-COVID reopening and has done less fiscal, so conditions aren't quite as strong.

> Two rows of three panels. United States: real GDP (vs a dashed potential line) fell about 12% in 2020 and is back to about the potential line (about +2%) in 2021, with a forward estimate dot at about +8% in 2022; core CPI is above its dashed trend (about +4.5% in 2021) with a forward estimate about +8%; the short rate (2010–2021) rose to about 2.4% in 2019, dropped to near 0% in 2020, and the discounted path rises only to about 0.5% by 2022–2023. Today's discounting is consistent with policy makers needing and being able to remain easy well into the future in order to achieve their desired outcomes.

**Bond and Short Rate Markets Are Pricing That Extremely Easy Policy Will Be Needed for a Decade and Won't Reach Its Limits**

The current pricing for short rates and bonds is consistent with a rerun of the last decade, where extremely easy monetary policies were both necessary and sustainable. Markets are priced for extremely low rates that will be needed to produce adequate growth rates, for inflation at or below central bank targets, and for these rates not to create unsustainable financial excesses. One difference between today and the last decade is that markets never fully discounted that reality back then (i.e., they consistently priced in more tightening than proved possible), and as a result, stimulation is more significant today. Despite experiencing a much stronger economy, higher inflation, and pockets of financial excesses, the current discounted range of outcomes for US nominal yields is consistent with only a slightly higher possibility of rates returning to pre-COVID levels than falling back to COVID lows. As noted in prior *Observations*, we think the odds are tilting toward rising inflation and excesses forcing the Fed's hand into raising rates faster than is currently priced in.

**Emerging Market Central Banks Are Now Tightening in Response to Higher Inflation, Less Stable Currencies, and the Desire to Not Fall Short of the Significant Tightening Priced In**

Emerging market central banks have had to deal with significantly more currency weakness, inflation, and a steeper yield curve, with markets pricing in that staying easy would not be sustainable. Choosing not to tighten in many of these cases risked further currency weakness and inflation. Tightening in line with pricing has been taking place, and tightening is priced in to continue despite many of these economies facing conditions that are much weaker than those in the US. Emerging market economies have been more mixed with their use of MP3. In some economies, like Mexico, fiscal was never used aggressively as a lever. By contrast, others, like Brazil, are aggressively pulling back after easing last year.

Because of the powerful effects of lower discount rates, equity pricing is still attractive relative to cash and consistent with only moderate earnings growth rates, particularly outside of the US. If rates stay this low for long, we see significantly more room for investors to continue to push further out the risk curve.

Today, the pricing is for a roughly normal range of outcomes. This contrasts with the range priced in prior to COVID, which was much narrower than usual. During the pandemic, implied volatilities were very high. Monday's equity decline of about 2% barely moves US equity prices from their highs.
